# How to read congressional stock disclosures.

Start with the asset, owner and trade date. Then check when the information reached you.

A congressional trade headline compresses a filing into a few words. The original record can tell a different, more limited story: a spouse’s transaction, a partial sale, a broad dollar range, or a trade made weeks before the document was signed. Here is how to read those fields using a real public House report.

## Open the original record.

A Periodic Transaction Report, or PTR, discloses certain securities transactions over $1,000 by covered filers, including reportable spouse and dependent-child activity. It is one type of disclosure; it is not a complete, continuously updated portfolio. The [House’s current instructions](https://ethics.house.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/7-8-2026-2025-Published-Instruction-Guide.pdf) explain the reporting scope and exclusions.

Use a tracker or newsletter to find an entry, then follow its link to the official filing. Confirm the document ID and check whether you are reading an initial report or an amendment. House records are available through the [Clerk’s disclosure site](https://disclosures-clerk.house.gov/FinancialDisclosure); Senate records have their own [official access page](https://www.senate.gov/legislative/Public_Disclosure/financial-disclosure.htm).

## A real example: a partial NVIDIA sale.

House filing [20034547](https://disclosures-clerk.house.gov/public_disc/ptr-pdfs/2026/20034547.pdf), signed by Representative Daniel Meuser, reports the following entry. This is a historical reading example, not a current trading recommendation.

### Asset

NVIDIA common stock, ticker NVDA. The filing’s **ST** asset code identifies stock.

### Owner

**SP**, meaning spouse. It identifies the reported owner; it does not establish who made the investment decision.

### Transaction

**S (partial)**: a partial sale. The record does not state the percentage of the position sold or how much remained.

### Amount

**$1,001–$15,000**. This is a reported range for the transaction, not an exact sale amount or a profit figure.

A faithful summary is: “The filing reports a spouse’s partial sale of NVIDIA stock on April 24, 2026, in the $1,001–$15,000 range.”

Calling it “Meuser sold $15,000 of NVIDIA today” would change the owner, turn a range into an exact amount and lose the trade date.

## Keep the dates separate.

- TransactionApril 24, 2026

The reported trade date.

- NotificationMay 12, 2026

The date of notification recorded in the filing.

- Digitally signedMay 21, 2026

The signature date printed on the report.

There are **27 calendar days** between the transaction and signature dates: 18 days to notification, then another 9 to signature. These are calculations from this one record, not an average delay across Congress.

**The PDF does not independently establish its web publication timestamp.** A signed date, a filing/index date, a portal release and a newsletter alert can be separate events. For a historical test, record when the information was actually public and available to the strategy. Do not credit a disclosure-based strategy with buying on the original trade date when it had not yet seen the disclosure.

The House filing deadline is the earlier of 30 days after awareness and 45 days after the transaction. Senate guidance uses written notification and the same 45-day outer limit. This is a filing rule, not a promise that every alert appears exactly 45 days later. Sources: [House deadline guidance](https://ethics.house.gov/periodic-transaction-report-calculator/) and [Senate FAQs](https://www.ethics.senate.gov/public/index.cfm/ethics-faqs).

## Read the amount and instrument carefully.

Keep the reported dollar band as a band. Using its upper endpoint or midpoint as the exact trade size adds information the filing did not provide. Without an execution price and share count, the range also cannot tell you how many shares changed hands.

For ownership, House codes include **SP** for spouse, **DC** for dependent child and **JT** for joint ownership. Ownership alone does not identify the person who placed or directed a trade.

A sale is not evidence of a short position. A partial sale is not a complete exit. An option also needs its own description: read any reported put/call type, strike and expiration rather than treating it as a purchase of the underlying shares. The dollar band alone does not describe an option’s exposure.

There are gaps by design. House instructions exclude several transaction types from PTR reporting, including mutual-fund and ETF transactions. Other disclosures may still apply. An absent PTR row therefore does not prove that a person has no investments or made no transactions. See the House guide’s PTR exclusions and column instructions, printed pages 14–16.

## Turn the filing into a research question.

- **Record what it says.** Keep the asset, reported owner, transaction type, dates, range and any amendment together. Preserve the source link.

- **Check the information’s age.** Separate an old trade from a newly surfaced record. Look at what became public between the transaction and your observation.

- **Research the company independently.** Read current earnings, cash flow, valuation assumptions and business risks. A disclosed transaction does not establish an investment thesis on its own.

- **Keep claims within the evidence.** The entry does not establish motive, present holdings, exact returns or wrongdoing. Any claim of predictive value needs a defined test using information available at the time, a benchmark and realistic costs.
